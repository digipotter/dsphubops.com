A van that breaks down mid-route costs more than the repair. You lose the route, pay for a tow or a recovery driver, and often disrupt the next day too. Preventive maintenance moves that cost to a time you choose.

This article lays out a simple structure you can adapt: who checks what and how often, how to schedule work around demand, and what to track.

Start with the manufacturer's schedule

Every van model has a maintenance schedule from its manufacturer, usually in the owner's manual or service guide. That schedule sets the exact intervals for oil, filters, transmission service, brakes, tires and other items, and it can change with how the van is used. Delivery work involves stop-and-go driving and heavy loads, which may fall under the manufacturer's severe service or heavy-use schedule, so read the section that matches your use.

This article does not give specific mileage intervals because they differ by make, model, engine and use. If your vans are leased or rented, check the agreement too, since it may spell out required service and who pays for it. Use the manufacturer's schedule as your baseline, then add checks on top of it based on what your own vans actually need.

Layer the checks by frequency

Different people can own different layers. The table below is a starting point.

How often Who What to check
Daily Driver Pre-trip and post-trip walkaround: tires, lights, brakes, mirrors, leaks, body damage, warning lights
Weekly Fleet lead or lead driver Tire pressure with a gauge, fluid levels, wipers, interior and cargo area condition, open defect reports
Monthly Fleet lead or mechanic Tire tread and wear patterns, brake wear, battery and terminals, belts and hoses, safety equipment, open recalls
By mileage or hours Shop or dealer Services listed in the manufacturer's schedule: oil and filters, brakes, transmission, tire rotation and others

To check open recalls, you can look up each van by its VIN on the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration's recall site or ask your dealer. Put the lookup on the monthly list.

Schedule work around demand

Pulling a van during a busy week costs a route. Pulling it on a light day often costs nothing. Look at your volume calendar and plan service for the slower stretches.

A few ways to do that:

  • Spread the fleet across the calendar so you are never servicing everything at once.
  • Book shop appointments early in the week or on lighter days, and ask for a drop-off time that fits your load-out.
  • Before peak, get every van current on its scheduled service so you head into the busy weeks with fewer surprises.
  • Keep a spare van or two in rotation where you can, so a service visit does not remove a route.

As an example only: a fleet of 30 vans might schedule four services a week, spread across lighter days. Those same four vans pulled on the same peak Monday could cost up to four routes. Use your own numbers.

Track work orders and grounded vehicles

A simple tracker beats memory. Keep one sheet or system that shows every van and its status. For each open work order, record:

  • Van unit number and the last six characters of the VIN.
  • The problem as reported, who reported it and the date.
  • Whether the van is safe to run, restricted or grounded.
  • The shop or mechanic, the parts ordered and the expected return date.
  • The date it came back and what was done.

Review the tracker every morning. A van that has been grounded for a week with no parts ordered is a problem someone needs to own. Also count days out of service per van over time. A van that keeps returning to the shop may cost more than it earns.

Keep records you can find later

Keep inspection reports, work orders, invoices and recall notices for each van in one place, whether that is a folder or a fleet system. These records help with warranty claims, resale, accident reviews, insurer questions and any audit by a delivery partner or agency.

How long you must keep them depends on your lease, your insurer, your delivery partner and any rules that apply to your vehicles. Ask them, and when in doubt, keep records for the time you own or lease the van and for some time after.

Review the program quarterly

Every few months, look at the tracker. Which repairs repeat? Which vans are out most often? Which service steps are getting missed? Adjust the checks and the calendar based on what you find.
