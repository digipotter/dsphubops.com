Drivers rarely quit over one thing. They leave because the job turned out different from the ad, the schedule kept shifting, or nobody noticed when something went wrong. Each of those is something a manager controls.

This article follows a driver through the points where staying or leaving usually gets decided, in the order they meet them.

Tell the truth in the ad and the interview

A new hire who learns the real job on day three feels misled. A candidate who hears it before accepting can decide for themselves, and the ones who say yes know what they signed up for.

Put the real details in the ad, then say them again in the interview:

  • Typical start time, how long a normal day runs, and how long a heavy day runs.
  • Stops and packages on a typical route, using your own recent numbers.
  • Weekend, holiday and peak season expectations.
  • Physical demands: lifting, stairs, time in and out of the van.
  • How pay works, including overtime and any bonus conditions.

Offer a realistic preview where you can. Let the candidate watch a load-out or ride along on a short route before they accept. Ask what a bad day looks like to them, then compare the answer with what you just described.

Give the first week a script

Too many new drivers get a uniform, a van and a "good luck." A fixed first week costs a few hours of manager time and removes most of the guesswork. Use the same plan for every hire:

  1. Day 1: Welcome, station tour, introductions to dispatch and a named buddy driver, app and device setup, a full vehicle walkaround.
  2. Day 2: Ride-along with the buddy driver, covering loading, sequencing and common problems.
  3. Day 3: A short route with the buddy riding along, so the new driver does the work and gets feedback.
  4. Days 4 and 5: A reduced route on their own, with dispatch checking in at midday.
  5. End of week: A ten-minute conversation about what went well, what was confusing and what they need.

Adjust the plan to your own training requirements and anything your delivery partner requires. What matters is that every new driver knows what happens each day and who to call.

Check in during the first 30 days

Put the check-ins on the calendar before the driver starts. A simple set: end of day 3, end of week 1, day 14 and day 30. Plan one more ride-along around week 2 to see how habits are forming.

Ask the same four questions each time:

  • What is going well?
  • What is harder than you expected?
  • Is there anything about the route, the van or the schedule we can fix?
  • Who have you been able to ask for help?

Write down what you hear and act on at least one item before the next check-in. If a driver raises a problem and nothing changes, they stop raising problems.

Make the schedule fair and predictable

Many drivers can handle a hard week better than an unpredictable one. Post the schedule on the same day every week. Tell drivers how much notice they can expect for changes, then keep to it.

A few habits help:

  • Rotate the hardest routes instead of parking the same people on them.
  • Take time-off requests in writing and answer within a stated number of days.
  • Apply the same rules for swaps, call-outs and overtime to everyone.
  • Say plainly how extra shifts are offered, so it never looks like favorites.

Some states and cities have their own rules on schedule notice, breaks and overtime. Check with your state labor agency or an employment attorney about what applies to you.

Recognize good work and coach before you discipline

Recognition costs almost nothing. Be specific: "No missed stops for two weeks" lands better than "good job." Say it the same day. Some drivers like a mention at the morning huddle and some do not, so ask.

Coaching works the same way. A driver who first hears about a problem in a written warning never had a chance to fix it. Talk about it the day it happens or the next day, in private, with the specifics. Save formal discipline for repeated problems after coaching and for serious violations your written policy names. Have an employment attorney review that policy.

Ask why people leave

Every driver who quits knows something you do not. Hold a short exit conversation in their last week, ideally with someone other than their direct supervisor. Ask what they liked, what made them decide to leave, and what one thing would have changed their mind.

Then keep a simple log with a reason category for each departure: pay, schedule, route, manager, vehicle, or something else. Review it monthly. Counts beat impressions, and if most recent departures name the same issue, start there.

Also talk to drivers who stay. Asking a veteran driver what keeps them here is often the fastest way to find what to protect.
