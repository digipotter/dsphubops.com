The records every delivery fleet should keep audit-ready

What to keep for each driver and vehicle, how to file it so it can be found in minutes, and a monthly routine that keeps it current.

4 minute read. Updated October 31, 2026.

An audit or inspection rarely arrives on a quiet day. It might come from an insurer, a program review, a payroll audit or a state agency, and the first request is usually the same: show me the record.

Records you can find in five minutes tell a reviewer your operation is under control. This article lists what to keep, a folder structure that makes it easy to find, and a monthly routine that keeps the files current.

Driver files

Keep one file per driver, created at hire and completed within the first week. Each file should hold:

  • A copy of the driver’s license, with the expiration date tracked on a separate list.
  • Background check authorization and results, kept confidential.
  • Motor vehicle record reviews, if you run them, with the date of each.
  • Training records: topic, date, trainer and the driver’s sign-off.
  • A signed offer letter or pay notice showing role and rate.

Keep Form I-9 in its own file, apart from the general driver file. It has its own retention rules and should be easy to produce on its own. Medical and drug-screen records, if you have them, belong in a restricted file too.

Vehicle files

Keep one file per vehicle, labeled by unit number and VIN.

  • Registration, and a copy of the insurance card or certificate showing the vehicle is covered
  • The lease, rental or purchase agreement
  • Inspection records: daily vehicle inspection reports and any periodic inspections
  • Maintenance and repair records: date, mileage, work done, who did it and the invoice
  • Damage and accident reports, with photos and the repair that followed

For every defect reported, show the date it was reported and the date it was fixed. A long gap between those two dates is what a reviewer is likely to ask about.

Safety, payroll and policy records

These records are not tied to one driver or one van, so keep them by year.

  • Safety: incident and accident reports with follow-up, near-miss notes, coaching records, and sign-in sheets from safety meetings.
  • Payroll and timekeeping: timecards with edit history, payroll registers, pay statements, break records where your state requires them, tax filings and deposits, and bonus or incentive plan documents.
  • Policies: the current handbook and every earlier version with the dates it was in force, plus attendance, safety, vehicle use and phone policies.

Every policy needs a signed and dated acknowledgment from each employee, tied to the version they received. An unsigned policy is hard to rely on when you need it.

A simple folder structure

Pick one structure and use it everywhere, on paper or on a shared drive. This one works for a small or mid-size fleet:

  • 01 Company: business licenses, insurance policies, program contracts
  • 02 Drivers
    • Active: one folder per driver, with subfolders for hiring, training, safety and signed policies
    • Former: the same folders, moved here with the separation date
  • 03 Vehicles: one folder per unit
  • 04 Safety: by year
  • 05 Payroll: by year and month
  • 06 Policies: current and archived versions
  • 07 Restricted: I-9 forms, background checks, and medical or drug-screen records, with limited access

Name files the same way every time: date first, then document type, then name, as in 2026-03-14_inspection_unit12. Dates first keep files in order.

Limit access to the restricted folder to the people who need it.

A monthly check routine

Set aside about an hour on the same day each month, and use the same steps every time.

  1. List everything that expires in the next 60 days: licenses, registrations, insurance, inspections and training.
  2. Check that every driver hired this month has a complete file.
  3. Pick three driver files and three vehicle files and check each against the lists above.
  4. Confirm each incident from the month has a report and a follow-up note.
  5. Confirm signed acknowledgments exist for any policy that changed.
  6. Move files for drivers who left into the Former folder.
  7. Back up the folders and review who has access to the restricted one.
  8. Write down the date, who did the check and what was missing.

The last step produces its own record. A dated log of monthly checks shows a reviewer that the routine actually happens.

Retention periods

How long to keep a record depends on the record type and the state. Payroll, tax, personnel, I-9 and vehicle records can each have their own period, and some must be kept after an employee leaves.

Ask your payroll provider, your insurer or an employment attorney for a retention schedule for your state. Then write it down and follow it, and do not discard records on a hunch.
