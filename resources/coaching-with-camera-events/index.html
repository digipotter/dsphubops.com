Using camera events to coach drivers fairly

A fair process for turning camera events into better driving: review in context, talk privately, write it down, follow up and recognize progress.

4 minute read. Updated October 31, 2026.

In-cab camera systems, Netradyne for example, flag events: a rolling stop, a close following distance, a moment of distraction. Treat each flag as a prompt to review, then decide what it means. How a manager handles that flag decides whether drivers see the camera as a coaching tool or as surveillance.

This article covers a fair process, from reviewing the clip to following up two weeks later.

Review each event in context

Do not coach from the event label alone. Watch the clip, including the seconds before and after. Systems make mistakes, and roads are messy. Ask what actually happened:

  • Did another driver cut in, or did a pedestrian or cyclist step out?
  • Was the driver doing something unsafe, or does the clip show a reasonable response to a hazard?
  • Is this a one-time event or the fourth similar event this month?
  • Was the camera right? If a flag is wrong, dismiss it and tell the driver.

Look at patterns over a few weeks, not single events. One bad moment on a hard day is different from the same behavior every shift. Review clips for every driver the same way, so the standard does not depend on who the manager likes.

Separate coaching from discipline

These are different tools. Coaching is about the next route: a specific behavior, a technique and a plan. Discipline is about consequences, and it belongs after repeated problems or serious violations.

Write the line down before you need it. Your policy should say which events lead to coaching, which lead straight to formal action, and what the steps are after a coaching conversation that does not work. Examples of events that may go straight to formal review include a collision, reckless driving or signs of impairment. Have an employment attorney review that policy.

Drivers should know the line ahead of time. Coaching only works when it is safe to be honest.

Run a short, private conversation

Five to ten minutes is enough. Do it in private, not at the dock in front of other drivers, and do it within a day or two of the event so it is still fresh.

  1. Open with the clip. Show it and say what you see, without adjectives.
  2. Ask for their view. "Walk me through what was happening." Listen first.
  3. Ask what is getting in the way. Time pressure, route design, a blind spot in the van or fatigue can all be fixable.
  4. Agree on one behavior. One change is easier to remember than five.
  5. Give a concrete technique. For example, a following distance cue or a full stop count at stop signs.
  6. Set a follow-up date. Before the driver leaves, say when you will check in.

Keep your tone calm. The aim is a driver who leaves knowing what to do differently, not one who feels caught.

Document what was said

Keep a short, factual record of every coaching conversation. Include the date, the event reviewed, what the driver said, what was agreed and the follow-up date. Stick to facts: "Rolling stop at intersection, reviewed clip, driver said sun glare hid the sign" is better than "driver is careless."

Have the driver acknowledge the conversation, by signature or in your system, and give them a copy. Records like this protect the driver and the company. If a coaching conversation is later followed by formal action, you can show that the driver was told clearly, given help and given time.

Follow up and recognize improvement

A coaching conversation without a follow-up is a lecture. On the agreed date, look at the driver’s events since the conversation and tell them what you found.

  • If it improved: Say so, specifically. Mention it at the huddle if the driver is comfortable with that.
  • If it did not: Ask what is still in the way, adjust the plan and set another date.
  • If the pattern continues: Move to the next step in your written policy, and keep the records in order.

Also use clips to recognize good driving: a smooth stop for a pedestrian, a safe response to a cut-in. If drivers only hear from you when something goes wrong, the camera will feel like a trap.

Be clear about privacy and consent

Rules on recording employees vary by state, including those covering audio, notice and consent, and how video or any facial or biometric data may be collected and stored. Talk to an employment attorney or your state labor agency about what applies where your drivers work.

Whatever the law requires, put your company policy in writing and have each driver sign it. It should say what is recorded, who can view clips, how long they are kept and how they are used in coaching and discipline. Review it with each new hire.
