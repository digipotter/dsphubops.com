Pre-trip and post-trip inspections: making them stick

What a good daily inspection covers, what to do when a driver finds a defect, and how to tell a real walkaround from a quick tap through the form.

4 minute read. Updated October 31, 2026.

A daily vehicle inspection takes a few minutes. Skipped, it can cost a day of routes, a damage dispute or an accident. Most drivers know how to inspect a van. What goes wrong is that the inspection feels like a form to get past before the real work starts.

This article covers what a good inspection includes, how to handle what drivers find, and how a manager can tell the difference between a walkaround and a tap-through.

Why daily inspections matter

Pre-trip and post-trip inspections, often called DVICs (daily vehicle inspection checklists), do three jobs. They catch safety problems before the van leaves the lot. They show when damage happened and who was driving. And they give your maintenance process an early warning, so a small issue gets fixed before it grounds the van.

Whether a written inspection report is required for your vehicles depends on their weight, how they are used and your state. Check with the relevant federal and state agencies, your insurer and your delivery partner. The habit is worth building either way.

What a good inspection covers

Keep the checklist short enough to finish and specific enough to mean something. Walk around the van in the same order every day:

  • Tires and wheels: Visible wear, cuts, bulges, low pressure, missing lug nuts.
  • Lights: Headlights, brake lights, turn signals, hazards, and any warning lights on the dash.
  • Brakes: Pedal feel, parking brake, any grinding or pulling when the van first moves.
  • Mirrors and glass: Cracks, loose mounts, clear view, working wipers and washer fluid.
  • Fluids and leaks: Spots under the van, obvious leaks, fluid levels where drivers are expected to check them.
  • Body damage: New dents, scrapes, broken panels, loose bumpers, with a note on anything that was not there yesterday.
  • Cargo area: Doors and latches, shelving, floor condition, nothing loose that could shift.
  • Safety equipment: Seat belt, horn, backup alarm or camera, fire extinguisher, first aid kit, warning triangles if your policy requires them.

The post-trip version is shorter: check for new damage, note any problem that showed up during the day, and report it before the driver leaves.

Use photos, and set rules for them

Photos turn a claim into a record. Ask for the same set each time: front, rear, both sides and the cargo area, plus a close-up of anything unusual. Make sure they are taken in daylight or with a light, show the whole panel, and are taken that day, not copied from a previous one.

Explain why. Photos protect the driver from being blamed for damage they did not cause. Drivers who see that benefit take better pictures.

Handle defects the same way every time

Drivers will stop reporting problems if reporting them gets them in trouble or costs them the route. Decide in advance what happens, write it down and stick to it.

  1. The driver reports the defect before leaving the lot, through the app or to dispatch.
  2. The dispatcher or fleet lead looks at it and makes the call: fine to run, fix first, or out of service.
  3. If the van is out of service, tag it, take the keys and log it in your tracker with the reason and the date.
  4. Move the driver to a spare van or reassign the route so the driver is not penalized for finding a problem.
  5. Close the loop: record the repair, who did it and when the van returned to service.

Safety-critical items such as brakes, steering, tires and lights should mean the van stays out of service until someone qualified clears it. Put your own list in writing, and base it on your manufacturer guidance and your insurer’s requirements.

Spot skipped or rushed inspections

You do not need to watch every walkaround. You need to look at the data and walk the lot a few times a week. Warning signs include:

  • Inspections completed in well under a minute, or all submitted at the same time as the route start.
  • The same drivers reporting "no issues" every day for weeks, while damage shows up on the return.
  • Photos that are blurry, repeated or taken in the dark.
  • Damage found on the return that was not noted in the morning.
  • A whole fleet with zero reported defects for a long stretch. Vans wear out, so a record that clean deserves a question.

Pair the data with spot checks. Pick a van at the dock, walk around it and compare what you see with what the driver reported. Do it casually and with different drivers each time, and thank people who catch things.

Make it part of the routine

Build the inspection into the start of the shift, with time allowed for it. If the schedule leaves no room, drivers will rush it. Do a short refresher at the huddle when you see a pattern, and recognize the driver who finds a real problem early.
