Payroll mistakes that cost delivery businesses money

Six places payroll goes wrong in a delivery business, and a short check for each one. Rules vary by state, so confirm yours.

4 minute read. Updated October 31, 2026.

Most payroll errors in a delivery business start before payroll runs. They start with a missed punch on a late route, a timecard edited without a note, or a bonus added after the hours were already entered.

Rules differ from place to place. Some states and cities add daily overtime, meal and rest break rules or local minimum wages on top of federal law, and others do not. Treat this article as a list of places to look, and confirm the details for each location you operate in with your payroll provider or an employment attorney.

Timecard edits and missed punches

Drivers forget to clock out when a route runs long, or they punch in at the wrong time and a manager fixes it later. Each of those edits changes pay, so each one needs a record.

  • Keep the original punch and the edited punch, with the name of the person who changed it and the reason.
  • Have the driver confirm any edit that changes their hours.
  • Run a missed-punch report before every payroll, not after.
  • Check that the punch window covers all paid work, including vehicle inspection, load-out and return processing.

Rounding practices can create wage claims if they consistently favor the employer. Ask your payroll provider whether yours is allowed in your state.

Overtime calculation

Start by confirming which overtime rule applies to your drivers: weekly, daily or both. Federal rules look at hours in a workweek, and some states add daily rules on top.

Then check how your payroll system is set up:

  • The workweek has a fixed start day and time, and it does not shift from one pay period to the next.
  • Overtime is calculated per workweek, even when a pay period covers parts of two weeks.
  • People who work at two rates, such as driver and dispatcher, are paid overtime at the correct rate for the combined work. Your payroll provider can confirm it.
  • Whether someone is exempt from overtime depends on duties and pay under federal and state rules, not on a job title.

Bonuses and incentives in the regular rate

Overtime is generally based on the regular rate of pay, and the regular rate is not always the same as the hourly wage. Bonuses tied to safety, attendance or performance that employees are promised in advance generally have to be included.

The common error is paying such a bonus on its own, outside the normal payroll calculation, so overtime is never recalculated.

Here is a simplified example using a weekly overtime rule only. An employee earns $20 an hour, works 50 hours and receives a $100 bonus that counts toward the regular rate. The regular rate becomes $22, so the half-time premium on 10 overtime hours is $110 instead of $100. The employee is short $10 for the week if the bonus is ignored.

The amount is small for one person in one week. Repeated across a crew and a year, it adds up. Your state may calculate this differently, so have your provider confirm how incentive pay should be handled.

Meal and rest break premiums

Some states require meal and rest breaks and add extra pay when a required break is missed, late or cut short. Many have no such requirement. If you run routes in more than one state, check the rules for each one.

On the road, breaks are hard to prove after the fact. Build the record into the day:

  • Record meal break start and end times where your state requires it.
  • Give dispatchers a way to see when each driver is due for a break.
  • Set up a separate pay code for break premiums so they show clearly on the pay statement.
  • Review break exceptions weekly, while the day is still easy to reconstruct.

New-hire setup errors

A setup mistake made on day one repeats every pay period until someone notices. Check each new record against the signed offer letter before the first payroll.

  • Pay rate, pay type and start date match the offer letter.
  • Overtime status is set correctly.
  • Federal and state tax forms are complete, and the work state is correct.
  • Pay frequency and direct deposit details are verified.
  • The employee exists once in the system, not twice.
  • The new-hire report is filed with the state on time.

Final pay timing

Final pay rules vary widely. Some states require payment on the day of separation. Others allow until the next regular payday, and the rule can depend on whether the employee quit or was let go.

Some states also require unused vacation or paid time off to be paid out. Keep a one-page sheet with your state’s rules and use it every time.

Start a separation checklist the same day someone leaves: confirm the last day worked, pull final hours, check for pending bonuses and have the final check ready by the date your state requires. Before deducting anything for unreturned equipment, ask your provider or an employment attorney whether your state allows it.
