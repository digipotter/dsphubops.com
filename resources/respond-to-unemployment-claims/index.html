How to respond to unemployment claims on time

A working routine for the first day a claim notice arrives, through the response, any appeal and the log you keep afterward.

4 minute read. Updated October 31, 2026.

When a former employee files for unemployment, the state agency sends the employer a notice asking for information. That notice has a due date, and everything in this article is built around meeting it.

Deadlines differ by state and are often short. Go by the date printed on your notice, not by anything you remember from a past claim.

This is general information, not legal advice. For a specific claim, contact your state workforce agency or an employment attorney.

Read the notice the day it arrives

Write down these items from the notice before you do anything else:

  • The claimant name and the claim or reference number
  • The date the notice was issued and the date your response is due
  • How to respond: online account, mail, fax or phone
  • Exactly what the agency is asking, such as wage information, the reason for separation, or both

Assign one person to own the response and put the due date on a shared calendar. Set an internal deadline a few days earlier, so a missing record does not push you past the real one.

Check that the agency has your current mailing address and contact for notices, and that someone looks at that mailbox and online account every working day. A notice that sits unopened in a stack of mail can use up most of the response time before anyone sees it.

Gather the records

Collect what answers the questions on the notice, and use documents that already existed. Never create or backdate a document after a claim arrives.

  • Timecards and schedules: for the final weeks of work, including any no-call or no-show dates.
  • Separation details: the last day worked, whether the employee quit, was discharged or was laid off, who made the decision and when, and what was said.
  • Written warnings: dated, signed where possible, with the policy cited.
  • Policy acknowledgments: the signed attendance, safety and conduct policies the employee received.
  • Messages: a resignation text or email, or notes from the separation conversation.
  • Payroll records: wages and pay periods, if the agency asks for them.

Put the documents in one folder labeled with the claim number, and number each page you plan to attach.

Write a factual response

Answer the question that was asked, in the order it was asked. Use dates, events and policies. Leave out opinions, labels and guesses about motive.

For example, "He was unreliable" is a judgment. "He did not report for his scheduled start on three dates, listed below, and received a written warning on the date shown" is a fact the agency can check against your attachments.

  • Match what you write to your separation paperwork and final pay records. Differences invite questions.
  • Do not say whether the person should or should not receive benefits. The agency decides that.
  • Refer to each attached document by name or number.
  • Respond even if you expect the claim to be approved. A missing response can mean the decision is made without your information.
  • Keep a copy of what you sent and the confirmation.

Appeals

When a determination arrives and you disagree with it, read it carefully. It will state how to appeal and by what date. Appeal windows are also short, so go by the date on the determination.

Decide quickly whether the decision matches the facts you can document. If it does not, file by the date given, then finish your preparation.

For a hearing, prepare these:

  • A one-page timeline of events with dates
  • The original documents, with copies for the other parties as the agency requires
  • Witnesses who saw events first-hand, such as the supervisor who issued the warnings
  • Answers that stick to what happened, not to what you think of the person

If the stakes are high or the case is complicated, talk to an employment attorney before the hearing.

Keep a claims log

A simple log gives you one place to see every open claim and every deadline. A spreadsheet works. Use these columns:

  • Claimant and claim number
  • Date the notice was received and the response due date
  • Person responsible
  • Date responded, and the confirmation number
  • Determination date and appeal deadline
  • Appeal filed (yes or no) and the outcome
  • Notes on what records were missing

Review the log once a month. If the same gap keeps showing up, such as missing policy acknowledgments, fix it at hiring.

If your state sends statements of benefits charged to your account, compare them against the log to catch claims you never saw.
